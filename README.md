## Start here

### What is cybersecurity?

Cybersecurity is the practice of protecting computers, networks, data, and the people who use them from theft, damage, disruption, or misuse. It looks less like the "hacker in a hoodie" from the movies and more like public health for technology: prevent problems, catch them early, respond well when something goes wrong, and help people make safer choices.

The field is much bigger than most people expect. Some cybersecurity professionals write code every day, and many never do. Some analyze network traffic. Others write policy, investigate crimes, audit companies, train employees, study attackers, or design secure hardware. **There is a door into this field for almost every major.**

### Is this for me?

Yes. This knowledge bank assumes **zero prior experience**, and every technical term is defined the first time it appears. Here are some natural starting points by major:

| If you study... | You might enjoy... |
|---|---|
| Business, accounting, finance | Governance, risk & compliance; security auditing; fraud prevention |
| Psychology, sociology, communications | Social engineering & the human side of security; security awareness; threat intelligence |
| Criminal justice, political science, pre-law | Digital forensics; privacy law & policy; threat intelligence |
| Biology, chemistry, health sciences | Healthcare privacy and security; medical device security; analytical work like malware analysis |
| Mathematics | Cryptography; data analysis for threat detection |
| Computer science, software engineering | Application & web security; cloud security; offensive security |
| Electrical & computer engineering | Hardware security; IoT & wireless; network security; OT/ICS |
| Industrial, mechanical, chemical engineering | OT/ICS and critical infrastructure security |

The guide [Choosing a Specialization](docs/guides/choosing-a-specialization.md) goes deeper.

### Your first five steps

1. **Skim the map.** Read the one-line descriptions in the table of contents below and open two or three areas that sound interesting.
2. **Learn the vocabulary as you go.** Keep the [Glossary](docs/glossary.md) open in another tab.
3. **Read [Ethics & Legality](docs/guides/ethics-and-legality.md) before you use any security tool.** The golden rule: *only test systems you own or have written permission to test.*
4. **Start with [Foundations](docs/areas/foundations.md).** Networking, operating systems, and the Linux command line help in every area, including the non-technical ones.
5. **Practice hands-on, for free.** See [Learning Platforms](docs/platforms/learning-platforms.md). Then come to a PandaHat meeting: we study together and compete as a team in CTFs (Capture the Flag competitions, which are legal hacking challenges).

**A realistic pace:** 3–5 hours a week, every week, beats cramming on one weekend.

### What you do *not* need

- A computer science major
- An expensive computer (a regular laptop is enough to start; see [Build a Home Lab on a Student Budget](docs/guides/home-lab.md))
- Money (every path lists free resources first, and paid resources are always optional)
- To already be "a hacker"

## How to use this knowledge bank

Every **area of emphasis** page follows the same ten parts, so once you've read one, you can navigate them all:

1. What it is
2. Why it matters
3. Who it fits
4. Prerequisites
5. Beginner study path
6. Free resources
7. Optional paid resources
8. Hands-on practice
9. Certifications
10. Career outlook

Resources are tagged so you always know the cost:

| Tag | Meaning |
|---|---|
| `[FREE]` | Completely free |
| `[FREEMIUM]` | Useful free tier, with optional paid upgrades |
| `[PAID – OPTIONAL]` | Costs money. Never required to follow any path here |

Pages marked 🚧 are still being written. Want to help? See [Contributing](#contributing).
